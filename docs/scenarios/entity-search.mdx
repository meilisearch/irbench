title: "Scenario: entity-search"
description: Entity search over a 1M-entity sample of DBpedia with graded human judgments (DBpedia-Entity v2)
---

Scenario id: `entity-search/dbpedia`. It tests the search a user runs to find
one named thing (a person, a place, a film, a band) or a short list of them,
in a collection where each record is a name and a short description. That is
the search box of an encyclopedia, a media or music catalogue, a directory, a
CRM or a knowledge graph. It is a common use of site search that the other
scenarios do not cover: three of them send long questions against long
documents, and WANDS has short queries but against a product catalogue.

## Source

- **Dataset:** DBpedia-Entity v2, from the `mteb/dbpedia` copy on Hugging Face
  (BEIR format), pinned to revision
  `7d8780d8ef0d0a55d13fa797fe39224ec806592c`. Reference: Hasibi et al.,
  *DBpedia-Entity v2: A Test Collection for Entity Search*, SIGIR 2017.
- **License:** MIT on the Hugging Face card and on the upstream repository
  (`github.com/iai-group/DBpedia-Entity`). The entity text comes from DBpedia
  2015-10, which is drawn from Wikipedia and licensed CC BY-SA 3.0.
- **Size:** 1,000,000 documents (a sample of 4,635,922 entities, see below),
  467 queries, 49,188 judgments (grades 0, 1, 2: 32,497, 9,809 and 6,882).

## Fields and modes

| Role | Fields |
|---|---|
| Searchable (priority order) | `title` (the entity name), `text` (the abstract) |
| Filterable, sortable, facets | none |

Modes: `keyword`, `semantic`, `hybrid`. Embeddings use `title` and `text`.
Names are short (median 2 words) and so are abstracts (median 47 words), so
almost none are truncated by the embedding model.

`title` comes first because the name is the strongest signal. A search box
that ranks a passing mention in an abstract above the entity itself would be
wrong. Each document also keeps its DBpedia URI in a `uri` field, which is
stored but not searched, so a result can be traced back.

### Why filtered, sorted and faceted are not run

The BEIR copy has only a name and an abstract per entity: no type, date,
category or number. Inventing a field (abstract length, the first letter of
the name) would test nothing a user does. Entity types would make these modes
meaningful, since filtering on `Person` or `Film` and faceting by type is a
real entity-search feature. They exist in DBpedia but would need a second
data source and a per-query choice of filter values. That is left for later.
The modes are absent from the scenario, not marked as unsupported per
engine, because no engine could run them.

## Queries

All 467 queries are kept: the 400 test and 67 dev queries of the BEIR copy,
the question-style ones included. The headline score is over all of them. The
four query groups of the DBpedia-Entity v2 paper are reported separately,
next to the overall scores:

| Group | Queries | Median words | Judged per query (mean) | Relevant per query (median) | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SemSearch ES | 113 | 2 | 66 | 9 | `glenn frey`, `bourbonnais il`, `mr rourke fantasy island`, `emery` |
| INEX-LD | 99 | 4 | 104 | 17 | `vietnam war movie`, `bicycle benefits health`, `guitar chord minor` |
| ListSearch | 115 | 6 | 96 | 28 | `Airports in Germany`, `Neil Gaiman novels`, `provinces and territories of Canada` |
| QALD2 | 140 | 8 | 146 | 12 | `Who designed the Brooklyn Bridge?`, `Which instruments did John Lennon play?` |

What each group is:

- **SemSearch ES:** the entity-name use case in its plainest form. Mostly a
  name, sometimes with a place or a word of context.
- **INEX-LD:** short keyword queries about a topic.
- **ListSearch:** queries that ask for a list of entities of one kind.
- **QALD2:** natural-language questions, like the knowledge-base scenarios.
  They are kept so the collection is used as published.

Over all 467 queries the median length is 5 words; 68 queries have two words
and 5 have one. Every query has at least one relevant entity; 62 have no
grade-2 entity. The judgments are deep: 105 judged entities per query on
average (median 88, range 21 to 1,499). Two thirds of them are grade 0, the
near misses that the original pooling kept, and they stay in the qrels as
judged non-relevant.

## Corpus

The full corpus has 4.64M entities. It does not fit the benchmark machine:
the bge-small vectors alone would need 6.7 GB (4.64M × 384 × 4 bytes), and
embedding them locally would take about 12 hours. The scenario therefore
indexes a sample, and the sample is part of the dataset's definition.

**Sampling rule.**

1. Keep every entity that is judged (any grade, either split) for any of the
   467 queries: 45,595 entities.
2. Add distractors drawn uniformly at random, without replacement, from the
   other entities, until the corpus holds 1,000,000 entities. The draw uses
   `random.Random(20261031)` over the non-judged entities in corpus file
   order.

The draw depends only on the pinned file, the seed and the size. Measured on
the real prepare run (2026-11-01): 1,000,000 documents, of which 45,595 are
judged and 954,405 are distractors, drawn from 4,635,922. No document and no
query was dropped, and no judgment was lost: every judged entity is in the
corpus.

**Why scores on the sample still mean something.**

- *Every judged entity stays.* The judgments came from pooling the top
  results of many 2017 systems. The grade-0 entities, 66% of the judgments,
  are the near misses those systems ranked high: the entities that compete
  hardest with the right answers. They are all in the sample, so the hard part
  of each query is intact.
- *Distractors are uniform.* They are not picked by BM25 or by an embedding
  model, so no engine or retrieval mode gets a sample built around its own
  ranking.
- *Engines are compared with each other, on one corpus.* Scores are higher
  than on the full corpus, because there are fewer unjudged entities to rank
  above the right answers. What IRBench reports is the comparison between
  engines on the same data.
- *Unjudged results shrink.* On the full corpus, an engine's top 10 often
  holds entities no one judged, which score as wrong. The sample drops most of
  them, so judged@10 goes up and the scores rest on human judgments more.

### Does the sample change who wins?

Before the scenario was built, BM25 was run over the full corpus and over
samples built by the same rule at several sizes, on all 467 queries. Two field
choices stood in for two engines: name and abstract together, and name only.
This is a lexical-only check. The script is a scratch tool outside the repo
and it used a different random draw than the shipped one (same rule, another
generator), so the table is evidence about the rule, not a measurement of the
shipped set. The ranker is a stand-in BM25, not one of the six engines.

nDCG@10 over the 467 queries, and judged@10:

| Corpus | Entities | name + abstract | name only | Gap | judged@10 (name + abstract) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Full | 4,635,922 | 0.274 | 0.259 | +0.015 | 0.61 |
| Sample | 2,000,000 | 0.294 | 0.272 | +0.022 | 0.70 |
| **Sample (the size used)** | **1,000,000** | **0.307** | **0.280** | **+0.027** | **0.77** |
| Sample | 500,000 | 0.317 | 0.290 | +0.027 | 0.83 |
| Sample | 250,000 | 0.324 | 0.293 | +0.031 | 0.87 |
| Sample | 100,000 | 0.329 | 0.299 | +0.030 | 0.90 |
| Judged pool only | 45,595 | 0.334 | 0.303 | +0.031 | 0.92 |

Per group, full corpus to 1M sample:

| Group | name + abstract | name only |
|---|---|---|
| SemSearch ES | 0.496 to 0.519 | 0.567 to 0.590 |
| INEX-LD | 0.239 to 0.275 | 0.209 to 0.235 |
| ListSearch | 0.185 to 0.221 | 0.130 to 0.146 |
| QALD2 | 0.194 to 0.229 | 0.152 to 0.170 |

What it shows:

- **The order holds.** At every size, overall and in every group, the same
  field choice wins. Name and abstract together win overall and on INEX-LD,
  ListSearch and QALD2. Name only wins on SemSearch ES, where queries are bare
  names and a passing mention in an abstract is noise.
- **Per query, the sample tracks the full corpus.** The Spearman correlation
  of per-query nDCG@10, full corpus versus 1M, is 0.97 (name and abstract)
  and 0.98 (name only). Of the 399 queries where the two choices score
  differently on either corpus, 352 (88%) have the same winner on both.
- **Scores rise by about a tenth** (0.274 to 0.307).
- **Gaps widen, and can become significant.** The gap between the two choices
  goes from +0.015 to +0.027. On the full corpus it is not significant
  (paired Wilcoxon, p = 0.17); at 1M it is (p = 0.01).

## Caveats

- **Scores are higher than on the full corpus.** Do not compare them with
  published DBpedia-Entity numbers or with scores from another corpus size.
  Compare engines with each other.
- **Gaps between engines may look larger, and more significant, than on the
  full 4.64M corpus.** The sample removes unjudged distractors that hurt every
  engine, so differences in how each one ranks the judged entities stand out
  more. This matters for how you read the report. The scenario page marks with
  ≈ every run that is not significantly below the best (a paired randomization
  test, p ≥ 0.05; see
  [Methodology](/methodology/overview#confidence-intervals-and-significance)).
  On this scenario, a run without ≈ is a real difference in ranking the same
  candidates, but the gap may be smaller on the full corpus, and a run with ≈
  may be a tie there too. The check above covers lexical ranking only.
- **The keyword-versus-semantic gap may be inflated.** The judgments were
  pooled from 2017 systems, mostly lexical. Entities that a dense model ranks
  high but nobody judged are more likely to be dropped by the sampling than
  those a lexical ranker ranks high. That can raise semantic scores relative
  to keyword scores on the same engine. It does not change the comparison
  between engines within a mode, since every engine searches the same text and
  the same vectors. This effect could not be measured: repeating the check
  for semantic search needs the full corpus embedded, which is the 12-hour
  cost that ruled it out.
- **Unjudged results.** An entity nobody judged scores as wrong. judged@10 is
  shown next to every nDCG@10. The sample raises it (0.61 on the full corpus
  to 0.77 at 1M, for the BM25 stand-in), but some of an engine's top results
  are still unjudged. When two runs are close and one has a much lower
  judged@10, the qrels may underrate it. See
  [Methodology](/methodology/overview#dataset-and-qrels-grades).
- **What the sample cannot show.** How an engine copes with millions of
  near-duplicate names (four people called `John Smith`). At 1M entities there
  are fewer of them than in a real catalogue.

## What was dropped

Nothing: no document, query or judgment was dropped (measured on the prepare
run of 2026-11-01).

## Perturbations

`typo` and `prefix`, seed `20261031`. People misspell names (`glen frey`), and
`prefix` stands for a name not typed to the end. There is no synonym
perturbation and no synonym list: no vocabulary was built for this scenario.

## Fit test and fallback size

No engine has indexed a million documents in IRBench before, so a fit test
runs before any scored run. For each of the six engines, on the `local`
profile and the default track:

- the index builds without an out-of-memory kill or container restart, within
  the scenario's `index_timeout_s` (2 hours);
- the relevance stage stays under the 0.1% error rate.

Peak container memory and build time are recorded per engine. If any one
engine fails, every engine uses the fallback size of 500,000 documents (the
`corpus_size` parameter of the dataset), after re-preparing and re-embedding.
A scenario compares engines on one corpus, so the size is never chosen per
engine. In the check above, 500,000 is almost as faithful as 1M (nDCG@10
0.317 against 0.307, judged@10 0.83 against 0.77). If the fallback is used,
the failure and its numbers will be stated here.
